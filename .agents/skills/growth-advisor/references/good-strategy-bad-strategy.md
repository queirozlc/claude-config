# Good Strategy / Bad Strategy — Richard Rumelt

> **Source warning:** This file comes from a 5-page getAbstract book abstract (getAbstract © 2014) of the 336-page book (Profile Books, 2011), about 2,600 words including page furniture and licence text. It is a third-party summary, not the book and not an excerpt of the author's own text. It covers the kernel (diagnosis, guiding policy, coherent action), the four marks of bad strategy, advantage and asymmetry, chain-link logic, strategy as design, growth, and strategy as hypothesis. It gives one or two short examples per idea. It omits the book's case studies, evidence, and most chapters. Every line below comes from that abstract only.
>
> One sentence: this book teaches you to tell a real action plan against a named problem apart from a list of goals and slogans.

## When to reach for this

- You wrote a "strategy" and it is a revenue target with adjectives.
- You must pick one market segment and drop the others.
- You plan a new product line and you are not sure it uses your strength.
- Your plan works only if every part improves at once.
- You feel pressure to grow by acquisition or by adding features.

## Core thesis

Good strategy is "strength applied to the most promising opportunity". It names the critical problem, sets one guiding policy, and orders concrete coordinated actions. Goals, vision, and motivation are not strategy. Bad strategy is easier to produce, so it is common. A good strategy is a hypothesis, an educated guess, and the market tests it.

## The playbook

The kernel of good strategy has three parts. Steps 1 to 6 execute it.

1. **Diagnose.** Ask what is going on. Then go further: find the patterns and facts that move your thinking into new areas. Name the single main challenge. If you cannot name the problem, you have no strategy.
2. **List your strategic ideas in writing.** A written catalogue turns concepts into actions. It also exposes your bias and blind spots.
3. **Attack your own idea with a "create-destroy" mind-set.** Do not keep the first insight. Build strong alternative views. Then try to tear each one apart and see which survives pressure.
4. **Use a "virtual panel of experts".** Imagine how people you respect would judge your view. Predict their objections. Rumelt names the late Steve Jobs as one of his.
5. **Write one guiding policy.** It is the overall method that directs all action. It is a signpost. It gives the reason for each action you take. Example from the abstract: Wells Fargo meets all client financial needs by cross-selling services.
6. **Design a set of coherent actions.** Make them consistent and coordinated. Allocate resources to them. Each action must build momentum for the others.

Two more rules from the abstract shape the design.

7. **Find your asymmetry.** Locate the difference between you and rivals that gives an advantage. Advantage often hides in what others call a weakness. Never compete where you have no edge.
8. **Check the chain links.** A system is only as strong as its weakest link. Strengthening strong units is wasted work while a bottleneck stands. Find the weak link before you spend. When all links are strong together, as at IKEA, rivals cannot copy the system.

Three properties mark a designed strategy.

9. **Premeditation.** Plan before you act. "Winging it is not a strategy."
10. **Anticipation.** Work out how competitors and others will act later.
11. **Design of coordinated action.** You construct a strategy. You do not pick one from a menu. Master strategists design novel responses instead of choosing standard options.

Then test it.

12. **Treat the strategy as a hypothesis and run it.** Success is the proof. Success also gives you proprietary knowledge that improves the next round.

### The four marks of bad strategy

The abstract names four characteristics.

1. **Fluff.** Empty slogans and buzzwords replace insight. Example: "Our fundamental strategy is one of customer-centric intermediation" means "our bank is a bank".
2. **Failure to face the challenge.** You never isolate the main problem. International Harvester redrew charts and never fixed poor employee relations.
3. **Mistaking goals for strategy.** Objectives with no concrete action steps are a wish list.
4. **Bad strategic objectives.** Aims that are not realistic and not feasible.

## Rules and heuristics

| Rule | Why | Failure mode if ignored |
|---|---|---|
| Name the one critical challenge before any plan | Strategy is problem-solving on fundamental issues, not ancillary ones | You produce a plan that never touches the real cause |
| Apply strength to the most promising opportunity | Advantage is the heart of strategy | You spread effort where you have no edge |
| Never compete where you hold no edge | This is "wrestling the gorilla" and it hands the advantage to someone else | You lose resources and your rival gains the upper hand |
| Do not extend into a business that needs different skills | The abstract's example: a new-fibre startup that starts a clothing company loses its advantage | You divert talent and resources and forfeit the original advantage |
| Decide what you will not do | "Strategy is at least as much about what an organization does not do as it is about what it does" | Effort scatters and no action reinforces another |
| Write one guiding policy, then derive actions from it | The policy gives the reason for each action | Actions conflict and cancel each other |
| Keep actions consistent and coordinated | Coordination creates momentum and a cascade of outcomes | Isolated initiatives produce no compounding effect |
| Fix the weakest link first | Chain-link logic: the system is as strong as its weakest link | Investment in strong areas returns nothing |
| Attack your own first idea before you commit | Judgment needs alternatives under pressure | You commit to the first plausible insight |
| Keep objectives practical and feasible | Bad strategic objectives are a mark of bad strategy | The plan fails and the team stops believing plans |
| Get in front of change early | The faster you "grab the high ground", the more success you get | Rivals mired in inertia still beat you to the shift |
| Work the granular details of your situation | Detail is where the jump on competitors comes from | Your plan stays generic and copyable |
| Treat the strategy as a hypothesis, not a certainty | "A leader does not need to get it totally right" | You over-invest in an untested guess |
| Chase value, not growth | Growth is the outcome of a successful strategy | You buy or build growth that adds no value |

## Anti-patterns

| Anti-pattern | What it looks like | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| The 20/20 plan | "Revenue up 20% a year, margin 20% or better", with cost and profit projections attached | Find the opportunity and the best way to pursue it, then set actions |
| Motivation as strategy | The leader believes ambitious goals will inspire the team to reach them | Add competencies and strategic insight; a rallying cry is not strategic |
| Fluff | "We will be the graphics arts services firm of choice"; "customer-centric intermediation" | Translate the sentence into plain English; if it says nothing, delete it |
| Dodging the problem | Reorganisation charts and analyses while the real cause goes unnamed | Name the main problem, then diagnose it |
| Goals without steps | A list of objectives with no concrete actions | Pair each objective with concrete action steps |
| Unrealistic objectives | Overarching aims that are not feasible | Set aims you can reach |
| Template thinking | Fill-in-the-blank strategy formats | Design a novel response; "you are customizing a performance race car, not choosing a souped-up vehicle from a car lot" |
| Following the crowd | Popular slogan replaces independent assessment | Assess the situation yourself and harness individual insight |
| Treating problems as negative thinking | Leaders see difficulties as pesky irritants and skip them | Acknowledge the difficult issue; it is the input to diagnosis |
| Engineering growth | Overpaying for acquisitions to make the growth number | Buy good businesses below worth, or improve them with your advantage; otherwise grow through innovation, demand and better offerings |
| Winging it | Action with no prior plan | Premeditate and anticipate |
| Strengthening the strong side | Investing in units that already work while a bottleneck stands | Identify bottlenecks and weak links first |

## Quotes worth keeping

> "A good strategy recognizes the nature of the challenge and offers a way of surmounting it. Simply being ambitious is not a strategy."

> "For many people in business, education and government, the word 'strategy' has become a verbal tic."

> "The truth is that many companies, especially large complex companies, don't really have strategies."

> "Strategy is at least as much about what an organization does not do as it is about what it does."

> "Good strategy is built on functional knowledge about what works, what doesn't and why."

> "A good strategy is, in the end, a hypothesis about what will work. Not a wild theory, but an educated judgment."

> "Growth is the outcome of a successful strategy, and attempts to engineer growth are exercises in magical thinking."

> "A leader does not need to get it totally right – the organization's strategy merely has to be more right than those of its rivals."

> "To see effective design-type strategy, you must usually look away from the long-successful incumbent toward the company that effectively invades its market space."

> "A high-quality strategic resource yielding a powerful competitive advantage makes for great strategic simplicity."

> "Good strategy grows out of an independent and careful assessment of the situation, harnessing individual insight to carefully crafted purpose. Bad strategy follows the crowd, substituting popular slogan for insights."

> "It is hard to show your skill as a sailor when there is no wind. Similarly, it is in moments of industry transition that skills at strategy are most valuable."

> "Strategy is about action, about doing something."

> "winging it is not a strategy"

## Applies to

Informed by this source:

- **Idea validation** — strong. Diagnosis, create-destroy, and strategy as hypothesis apply directly.
- **Positioning** — strong. The Stephanie example is a segment choice: serve the affluent, time-pressed professionals and not the cost-conscious students. Asymmetry and "do not compete without an edge" are positioning rules.
- **Product decisions** — strong. Coherent action, chain-link logic, and the fibre-startup example govern what you build and what you refuse.
- **Growth** — moderate. The source says growth follows a working strategy, and warns against engineering growth. It gives no growth tactics.
- **Retention / churn** — weak, indirect only. Stephanie's actions target one segment's needs.
- **Pricing** — not covered.
- **Landing page / CRO** — not covered, except that fluff copy is a bad sign.
- **Onboarding / activation** — not covered.

## Conflicts and limits

- This is a summary of a summary. Treat it as a checklist, not as evidence. The abstract asserts conclusions and gives almost no data.
- The abstract gives no method to run a diagnosis. It says to find patterns and facts. It does not say how.
- It gives no test to tell a real guiding policy from a slogan, beyond the plain-English translation trick.
- It contains no SaaS, software, or startup case. The examples are a graphics firm, a bank, a heavy-equipment maker, a corner grocery, Wells Fargo and IKEA. All are large or offline.
- It says "never engage in any form of competition in which you hold little or no edge". It does not say how to build an edge when you start with none.
- It says growth is an outcome, not a target. It does not reconcile this with running a business that must hit revenue numbers.
- It omits the book's chapters, frameworks, and history. If you want the reasoning, the evidence, or the full list of bad-strategy examples, buy the book.

# Influence: The Psychology of Persuasion — Robert B. Cialdini, Ph.D.

> A field manual for the six shortcuts that make people say yes without thinking, written so you can use them honestly and refuse them when others fake them.

## When to reach for this

- You write landing page copy, pricing pages, or trial emails and want to know why a line converts.
- You design onboarding and need users to act, not just read.
- You suspect a growth tactic is manipulative and want a test to decide.
- A competitor uses a tactic on your buyers and you want to name it.
- You negotiate a deal, a salary, or a contract and want to see the other side's structure.

## Core thesis

- People cannot analyze every request. They use shortcuts.
- Each shortcut is a rule that is usually right. The source calls these rules weapons of influence.
- A single trigger feature can fire the whole rule. The author calls this "click, whirr."
- Compliance professionals learn the trigger features and pull them on purpose.
- The tactic works like jujitsu. The practitioner supplies almost no force. The rule supplies the force.
- Six principles cover most tactics: reciprocation, commitment and consistency, social proof, liking, authority, scarcity.
- The author leaves out material self-interest on purpose. He calls it a motivational given, not a separate principle.
- Victims usually credit their own free choice, not the practitioner. That is why the tactics stay invisible.
- The defense is never to kill the shortcut. The defense is to detect a counterfeit trigger and then refuse.
- The source separates honest practitioners from exploiters. Honest practitioners are called allies. Exploiters are those who "falsify, counterfeit, or misrepresent the evidence."

## The principles

### 0. The contrast principle

The source presents this in Chapter 1 as a weapon of influence in its own right, before the six.

**Mechanism.** We judge a second item against the first. If the second differs from the first, we see the difference as larger than it is. The source demonstrates this with three pails of water, with suit-then-sweater selling, and with real estate "setup" houses shown before the real listing.

**Trigger conditions.** Two items presented in sequence. The buyer has no independent anchor for value. The seller controls the order.

**Honest application in SaaS.** Show your highest tier first on the pricing page, then the tier you expect most buyers to take. Show the cost of the problem before the cost of the fix. Both are true statements presented in a useful order.

**Manipulative misuse.** Invent a decoy you never intend to sell, priced only to make the real item look small. The source describes exactly this: run-down "setup" houses kept on the list to be shown, never sold. Also the car dealer who adds options one at a time after the big number is set.

**Defense.** Price the second item alone. Ask what you would pay for it if you had seen nothing first.

### 1. Reciprocation

**Mechanism.** We repay what another person gives us. The source states the rule as: "we should try to repay, in kind, what another person has provided us." The debt is unpleasant. We often repay more than we received to end the discomfort. Refusing to repay earns labels the source lists: moocher, ingrate, welsher.

Two forms appear:

1. **Gift then request.** The Regan experiment. A ten cent Coca-Cola produced twice the raffle ticket sales. Liking for the requester stopped mattering once the gift landed.
2. **Reciprocal concession, called rejection-then-retreat.** Ask large, get refused, then retreat to the request you wanted. The zoo-chaperone study moved compliance from 17 percent to 50 percent. The technique also raises follow-through (85 percent versus 50 percent show-up rate) and willingness to agree again later (84 percent versus 43 percent), because targets feel responsible for and satisfied with the deal they think they shaped.

**Trigger conditions.** The gift arrives uninvited. The recipient cannot easily refuse it. The giver chooses both the first favor and the repayment. The initial request is large but not absurd. Research at Bar-Ilan University showed the tactic backfires when the first demand looks unreasonable.

**Honest application in SaaS.** Give real value before you ask. A working free tier. A template, a calculator, or an audit the user keeps whether or not they buy. Genuine free support during a trial. On negotiation: open above your target, then concede in real steps you can defend.

**Manipulative misuse.** The gift that is really a sales device. The source's example is the free home fire-safety inspection that exists to sell an overpriced alarm system. In SaaS this is the "free audit" that is a scripted pitch, or the free plan built to be unusable so the upgrade prompt fires. Also the fake concession: an inflated list price you never charge, "discounted" to your real price.

**Defense.** Redefine the gift. The source is explicit: "the rule says that favors are to be met with favors; it does not require that tricks be met with favors." Accept a real favor and accept the obligation. When you decide the first move was a compliance device, the obligation ends.

### 2. Commitment and consistency

**Mechanism.** Once we take a stand, we behave in ways that justify it. Bettors grow more confident about a horse right after buying the ticket. The commitment then grows its own supports. The person invents new reasons, so the original reason can be removed and the choice still stands.

Commitments bind hardest when they are:

- **Active.** The person does something, not just hears something.
- **Public.** The Deutsch and Gerard line-judging study: public writers held their wrong answers hardest, private writers next, uncommitted subjects least.
- **Effortful.** Aronson and Mills: a severe initiation made a dull group seem valuable.
- **Freely chosen.** Freedman's robot study. A mild reason produced lasting change. A strong threat produced obedience only while the threat was present. Big rewards and strong threats give the person an external excuse, so no inner change occurs.

Named tactics: **foot-in-the-door** (Freedman and Fraser; a three-inch BE A SAFE DRIVER sticker made 76 percent accept a huge lawn billboard later), **lowball** (offer a good deal, let commitment form, then withdraw the advantage), and the survey commitment ("How are you feeling this evening?" doubled donation compliance in Howard's Dallas study).

**Trigger conditions.** A small, cheap, apparently trivial first yes. Something written. Something visible to others. Little visible outside pressure.

**Honest application in SaaS.** Ask for a small real action in onboarding before the big one: connect one data source, name one goal, invite one teammate. Let users state their own goal in their own words, then show progress against it. Publish a changelog and keep it. Pallak's Iowa energy study is the honest version: a public commitment produced 12.2 percent savings, and savings rose to 15.5 percent after the incentive was withdrawn.

**Manipulative misuse.** Lowball pricing. Advertise a price, let the user complete setup and import data, then reveal the required add-on. Confirmshaming that forces a self-labelling click ("No, I do not care about growth"). Contests and testimonials engineered only to get users on record praising the product. The source's darkest case is the Chinese POW camp program: start small, get it written, get it public, keep rewards small so the man owns the act.

**Defense.** Two signals. First, the stomach. It tightens when you know you are being cornered by your own earlier words. Second, the heart of hearts. Ask: "Knowing what I now know, if I could go back in time, would I make the same choice?" Trust the first flash of feeling, before the justifications arrive.

### 3. Social proof

**Mechanism.** The source states it plainly: "one means we use to determine what is correct is to find out what other people think is correct." We judge behavior as more correct as we see more people doing it. Canned laughter works even when everyone can hear that it is fake.

**Trigger conditions.** Two amplifiers, both named in the source:

1. **Uncertainty.** When the situation is ambiguous, we look at others. This produces pluralistic ignorance. In the seizure study, a lone bystander helped 85 percent of the time; with five bystanders, 31 percent. With two coached passive bystanders, smoke was reported only 10 percent of the time.
2. **Similarity.** We copy people like us. The dropped-wallet study: 33 percent returned when the prior finder seemed foreign, 70 percent when he seemed similar. Phillips's suicide research showed imitation matched the victim's age group.

**Honest application in SaaS.** Show real counts, real logos, real reviews. Segment the proof so the visitor sees their own kind of company. Testimonials from a similar role beat testimonials from a famous stranger. In onboarding, show what similar teams configured first. When you need one person to act, name that person and the exact task. The source's emergency advice transfers directly: "You, sir, in the blue jacket, I need help. Call an ambulance." Diffuse asks get diffuse results.

**Manipulative misuse.** Fabricated proof. The source names claques in opera, bartenders salting tip jars, discos manufacturing lines outside empty rooms, seeded revival crowds, and actors playing "unrehearsed" consumers. In SaaS this is fake live-activity popups, purchased reviews, and invented user counts. The racetrack Reader's Report shows the second failure mode: proof can snowball off a single planted bet and be wrong.

**Defense.** Treat social proof as an autopilot. Keep it engaged. Glance at the instrument panel. Look for two failure modes: proof that was faked, and proof that snowballed from an innocent error, as with the freeway lane switchers. The source recommends active retaliation against faked proof: do not buy, and tell the manufacturer why.

### 4. Liking

**Mechanism.** We say yes to people we like. The source lists the causes:

- **Physical attractiveness.** A halo effect. Attractive candidates got more than two and a half times the votes in a Canadian election study. Voters denied any influence.
- **Similarity.** Dress, opinions, background, interests. Salespeople are trained to mirror and match.
- **Compliments.** In the North Carolina study, pure praise produced liking even when subjects knew the flatterer wanted something, and even when the praise was untrue. Joe Girard mailed "I like you" to over 13,000 customers every month.
- **Contact and cooperation.** Familiarity helps, but only under pleasant conditions. Repeated contact under competition increases dislike. Sherif's camp study and Aronson's jigsaw classroom show that shared goals convert rivals into allies.
- **Association.** We transfer feelings from a connected thing. Weather forecasters get hate mail. Razran's luncheon technique made political statements more agreeable when eaten alongside food. Fans say "we won" and "they lost."

**Trigger conditions.** Any personal contact before the ask. Warmth arriving faster than the relationship warrants.

**Honest application in SaaS.** Real referral programs, because a request from a friend carries the friendship. A real founder with a real face and a real story, so similarity can attach to something true. Specific, accurate praise of the user's work. Position yourself as a teammate against the actual shared problem, and then actually be one.

**Manipulative misuse.** Manufactured similarity. The source warns to "be careful around salespeople who just seem to be just like you." Fake teammate framing. Good Cop/Bad Cop, where one party pretends to fight your corner against an invented opponent. The source notes that in the dealership he infiltrated, the salesman who "battled the boss" often did not speak to the boss at all. MCI's Calling Circle is the pure form: it converts a friendship into a switching request, and it "works nine out of ten times."

**Defense.** Watch the effect, not the causes. There are too many causes to block. Ask one question: have I come to like this person more quickly or more deeply than I would have expected? If yes, separate the person from the deal. You drive the car home, not the salesman.

### 5. Authority

**Mechanism.** We defer to legitimate authority, often without thinking. In Milgram's obedience studies about two thirds of subjects delivered every shock to the maximum. Milgram's conclusion: "It is the extreme willingness of adults to go to almost any lengths on the command of an authority that constitutes the chief finding of the study."

The deference attaches to **symbols**, not to substance. The source names three:

- **Titles.** In the midwestern hospital study, 95 percent of nurses prepared a dangerous, unauthorized, phoned-in overdose because the caller claimed the title "doctor." A visitor's perceived height grew about half an inch per step of academic rank.
- **Clothes.** Bickman's uniform studies. Nearly all pedestrians obeyed a man in a guard uniform who had already walked away; fewer than half obeyed him in street clothes. Three and a half times as many people followed a suited jaywalker as a casually dressed one.
- **Trappings.** Motorists honked at an economy car almost every time and waited behind a luxury car half the time without honking.

People consistently underestimate all of these effects on themselves.

**Trigger conditions.** A symbol of expertise present. The domain feels technical. The user has no independent way to check.

**Honest application in SaaS.** State real credentials and make them relevant. Publish your security posture, your benchmarks, and your methodology so the claim can be checked. Cite named experts who actually reviewed the work. Use the trustworthiness move honestly: name a real limitation of your product before you name the strengths. The source's waiter Vincent recommended cheaper dishes and gained credibility that carried the wine order. Say plainly which customers should not buy.

**Manipulative misuse.** Borrowed authority with no substance. The source's example is an actor who played a doctor selling coffee. In SaaS this is invented advisory boards, security badges you did not earn, fake certifications, and "as seen in" logos for a paid placement. The bank-examiner scam shows the endgame: a suit plus a uniform, and no authority at all.

**Defense.** Two questions. "Is this authority truly an expert?" Check the credentials and their relevance to this topic. Then "How truthful can we expect the expert to be here?" Ask how the expert gains from your compliance. Watch for the small self-criticism that buys credibility for the large claim.

### 6. Scarcity

**Mechanism.** The source defines it as: "opportunities seem more valuable to us when their availability is limited." Two engines drive it. First, scarce things are usually better, so scarcity is a decent quality shortcut. Second, psychological reactance, from Jack Brehm. When a freedom is threatened, we want the thing more. The source notes that loss framing beats gain framing: homeowners insulate more when told what they will lose.

Reactance shows up as the terrible twos, the Romeo and Juliet effect, Miami's phosphate-detergent hoarding, and juries that weight evidence more after a judge rules it inadmissible.

**Trigger conditions.** From Worchel's cookie study, three conditions in rising order of power:

1. Constant scarcity beats abundance.
2. **New** scarcity beats constant scarcity. A drop from plenty to little is strongest. Davies applied this to revolutions: revolt follows a sharp reversal after improvement, not steady deprivation.
3. **Scarcity plus competition** is strongest of all. Cookies made scarce by other people's demand rated highest. Barry Diller overpaid by about a million dollars for one movie showing in the first open network auction.

Scarcity applies to information too. Brock and Fromkin's commodity theory. In the beef-importer experiment, scarcity news doubled orders, and *exclusive* scarcity news multiplied them by six.

**Honest application in SaaS.** Real cohort limits for a beta you can only support at that size. Real founding-customer pricing that really ends. Real seat limits. Real deprecation dates. Genuinely exclusive information for a paying tier or a private beta, where the exclusivity is a fact. Loss framing on truthful stakes: what data or ranking the customer loses by waiting.

**Manipulative misuse.** Fabricated scarcity. The source names the limited-number tactic where "sometimes the limited-number information was true, sometimes it was wholly false." The deadline tactic. The can't-come-back claim the sales manager admitted was nonsense. The appliance sold at the moment it seems gone, with the customer asked to commit before stock is "found." His brother Richard's method of booking every buyer for the same appointment time is manufactured competition. In SaaS: evergreen countdown timers that reset, permanent "3 seats left," fake flash sales.

**Defense.** Two stages. Use the rise in arousal itself as the stop signal, because scarcity suppresses the thinking you would need to detect it. Then ask why you want the item. If you want it to own it, scarcity is a fair guide to price. If you want it to use it, remember the finding: the scarce cookies were rated more desirable but not better tasting. "The joy is not in experiencing a scarce commodity but in possessing it."

## Rules and heuristics

| Rule | Why | Failure mode if ignored |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Give something real before you ask. | Reciprocation runs on received value, not on promised value. | Your "gift" reads as a sales device, and the obligation never forms. |
| Open above your target and concede in visible steps. | Reciprocal concessions raise agreement, follow-through, and satisfaction. | You anchor at your target and get negotiated below it. |
| Keep the first ask reasonable. | Bar-Ilan research: an absurd first demand kills the retreat. | The retreat reads as a trick and gets no concession back. |
| Ask for a small, active, freely chosen first step. | Active, public, effortful, uncoerced commitments change self-image. | Users read your docs, never act, and never form a habit. |
| Keep incentives small enough that the user owns the act. | Large rewards give an external excuse, so no inner change occurs. | Behavior stops when the discount stops. |
| Let onboarding cost some real effort. | Effort raises valuation of what it bought. | A frictionless setup produces a user with nothing invested. |
| Segment social proof by visitor type. | Similarity multiplies social proof. | Enterprise logos convince no solo founder. |
| Name one person and one task when you need action. | Diffuse requests spread responsibility to nobody. | A team-wide email produces zero owners. |
| Argue once against your own interest, with a real limitation. | It establishes trustworthiness before the main claim. | Every claim reads as sales copy and none lands. |
| Check the expert's relevance and their stake. | Symbols of authority are easier to fake than authority. | You copy a tactic from someone with no evidence and a book to sell. |
| Make scarcity true or do not use it. | Fabricated scarcity is a counterfeit trigger, which the source treats as the offence. | Users detect the reset timer once and discount every future claim. |
| Never remove an established freedom quietly. | Reactance is strongest against a freedom people already hold. | A pulled free tier costs more goodwill than never offering one. |
| Enforce limits consistently. | Erratic enforcement creates a freedom, then revokes it. | Inconsistent rules produce rebellious users, as they produce rebellious children. |
| Treat rising arousal as a stop signal. | Scarcity and competition suppress the analysis you need. | You bid like the CBS president who kept raising with no plan. |
| Separate the person from the deal. | Undue liking is the most common source of large mistakes. | You sign with the likable vendor and the worse contract. |

## Anti-patterns and dark patterns

Dark patterns are a failure mode of these principles, not a tactic set. In every row the trigger is real and the evidence behind it is counterfeit. The source calls the counterfeiter the enemy and the honest practitioner an ally.

| Pattern | What it looks like | Why it backfires | Honest alternative |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Evergreen countdown | A timer that resets on reload, or "offer ends today" every day. | One reload exposes it. The scarcity shortcut is disabled for all your future claims. | A real deadline tied to a real cohort, capacity, or price change. |
| Fake stock counter | "Only 3 seats left" on unlimited SaaS seats. | The claim is checkable and false. It converts a quality signal into a lie. | Publish the real cap and the real reason for it. |
| Manufactured social proof | Invented live-activity popups, bought reviews, actor testimonials. | The source shows fakery is usually visible, and visible fakery invites retaliation. | Fewer real customers, named, with checkable detail. |
| Confirmshaming | Decline links worded "No thanks, I like wasting money." | It forces a self-labelling commitment the user resents. Resentment attaches to you. | A neutral decline. Ask for a small honest commitment instead. |
| Price lowball | Advertise a price, reveal required add-ons after setup and data import. | It works once. It is the car-showroom lowball, and it trades a customer for a transaction. | Show total cost before the user invests effort. |
| Decoy tier nobody may buy | A tier priced only to make the middle tier look small. | It is the run-down "setup" house. If a buyer picks it, you must honor it. | Order real tiers largest first. Every tier must be a real offer. |
| Roach motel cancellation | Signup takes one click, cancellation takes a phone call. | It removes an established freedom, which is the strongest trigger for reactance. | Self-serve cancellation. Ask for the reason after, not before. |
| Fake teammate | "Let me fight with my manager for your discount," with no manager. | It is Good Cop, and the source documents that the fight often never happens. | Publish the discount policy. Then you have nothing to stage. |
| Borrowed authority | Advisory board of people who never advised. Badges you did not earn. | It is the actor playing a doctor. One check by one prospect ends it. | Real credentials, real audits, real named reviewers. |
| Weaponized friendship | Referral flows that make declining an insult to a friend. | It converts the friendship into the cost of saying no, as the MCI Calling Circle did. | Reward both sides and make declining costless and private. |
| Unrefusable gift | Onboarding credit or gift that cannot be declined, then a hard ask. | It is the Krishna flower. Many pay and then discard the gift and the relationship. | Make the gift genuinely optional and genuinely useful. |
| Manufactured bidding war | Fake rival buyers, fake demand spikes to force a decision now. | The source calls this "goosing 'em off the fence." Buyers who learn of it never return. | Real demand data. Let the buyer take the time to think. |

## Quotes worth keeping

Quoted exactly from the source.

1. "civilization advances by extending the number of operations we can perform without thinking about them." — Whitehead, quoted in Chapter 1. Use when defending shortcuts as necessary, not stupid.
2. "They make us terribly vulnerable to anyone who does know how they work." — On automatic patterns. Use to open any internal talk on persuasion ethics.
3. "We are human because our ancestors learned to share their food and their skills in an honored network of obligation," — Leakey. Use to justify giving real value before asking.
4. "There is an obligation to give, an obligation to receive, and an obligation to repay." — Mauss. Use when explaining why an unrequested gift still creates debt.
5. "A favor rightly follows a favor—not a piece of sales strategy." Use as the personal test for accepting a vendor's freebie.
6. "It is easier to resist at the beginning than at the end." — Leonardo da Vinci, chapter epigraph. Use when arguing against a small first concession in a negotiation.
7. "A foolish consistency is the hobgoblin of little minds." — Emerson, restored to the full wording. Use when killing a feature you committed to publicly.
8. "It states that one means we use to determine what is correct is to find out what other people think is correct." — Definition of social proof. Use in a positioning doc.
9. "Since 95 percent of the people are imitators and only 5 percent initiators, people are persuaded more by the actions of others than by any proof we can offer." — Cavett Robert. Use to argue for a testimonials budget over a features section.
10. "It is the extreme willingness of adults to go to almost any lengths on the command of an authority that constitutes the chief finding of the study." — Milgram. Use when building any approval or escalation flow.
11. "The joy is not in experiencing a scarce commodity but in possessing it. It is important that we not confuse the two." Use before you buy the tool everyone is fighting over.
12. "The proper targets for counteraggression are only those individuals who falsify, counterfeit, or misrepresent the evidence that naturally cues our shortcut responses." Use as the one-line ethics test for any tactic you ship.

## Applies to

- **Idea validation.** Weakly. The book explains why people say yes to a request. It does not tell you whether a market exists. Warning: social proof and liking can make a friendly interview sound like demand. Ask for a costly commitment, not a compliment.
- **Positioning.** Strongly, through authority and similarity. Say who you are for, in their words. Relevance of credentials beats volume of credentials.
- **Pricing.** Strongly, through contrast, scarcity, and reciprocation. Order tiers highest first. Anchor on the cost of the problem. Use loss framing on real stakes. Every anchor must be an offer you would honor.
- **Landing page and CRO.** The densest application. Segmented social proof. Real authority markers. One clear, named action. Honest scarcity only. The "argue against your own interest" move works well in a comparison page.
- **Onboarding and activation.** Strongly, through commitment and consistency. Ask for a small active step. Let the user state their own goal. Keep incentives small so the user owns the behavior. Some effort is a feature.
- **Retention and churn.** Strongly, through consistency and reactance. Users defend habits they built. Never remove an established freedom without warning and a path. Erratic enforcement of limits creates rebellion.
- **Growth.** Strongly, through liking and social proof. Referrals carry the friendship. Give something real for free. Do not make declining an insult.
- **Product decisions.** Moderately. Two warnings from the book. Public commitments make you defend bad choices, so use secret ballots for close calls, as the jury research suggests. Competition for a contested resource clouds judgment, so treat your own arousal as a stop signal in a bidding or hiring race.

## Conflicts and limits

**What the source does not cover.**

- No positioning, no pricing model, no unit economics, no product strategy, no channel strategy. Every SaaS application in this file is my extension of the principles, not the author's advice.
- No digital interfaces. This edition predates the consumer web. The examples are door-to-door sales, car lots, telephone solicitation, and television.
- No treatment of unity, or of pre-suasion, which the author added in later work. This edition names six principles plus the contrast principle.

**The detectives, smugglers, bandits framing.** This source text does not use those words. I checked. The distinction it does draw is between compliance professionals "who play fairly by the rules of shortcut response," whom the author calls "our allies," and the exploiters who "falsify, counterfeit, or misrepresent the evidence." I used the source's own wording. If you need the detective, smuggler, and bandit language, it comes from another book.

**Replication concerns.** The source presents these findings as settled. Many are not, by later standards.

- Several key studies are single experiments with small samples reported in the 1960s and 1970s. The source rarely gives sample sizes or effect sizes.
- The Kitty Genovese account, given here as thirty-eight witnesses who did nothing, has been contested by later journalism. The bystander effect itself replicates better than the anecdote does.
- Phillips's Werther effect, the imitation-suicide analysis linking front-page suicides to airline and car crashes, is an archival correlation. The book treats it as proven. It is contested.
- The book is written to persuade. It uses the principles it describes: authority citations, vivid social proof, and a likable narrator. Read it that way.

**Cultural limits.** The author claims reciprocity is universal, citing Gouldner. The other principles are demonstrated almost entirely on Americans, with a few Canadian, Israeli, Australian, and Dutch studies. Obedience results are reported as similar in Holland, Germany, Spain, Italy, Australia, and Jordan. Nothing here tests East Asian, African, or Latin American markets systematically. Norms about gifts, flattery, and authority vary. Do not assume a tactic transfers.

**Where the advice is contested inside the book.** The author admits cooperative learning needs much more research before anyone can say how often, at what age, and in which groups it works. He also notes that competition has real value and should not be eliminated. He is honest that his lowball observations in the car showroom are subjective, and points the reader to controlled studies instead.

**Source text quality.** The extraction is clean and complete, front matter through index. Defects found:

- Chapter opening lines are scrambled. The first sentence of most chapters has its clauses transposed, for example "A ment. He sent Christmas cards to a sample of perfect strangers. FEW YEARS AGO, A UNIVERSITY PROFESSOR TRIED A LITTLE experi-".
- Occasional stray hyphens inside words from line-break artifacts: "percep-tions", "uncon-vincingly", "psychològy".
- Chapter 3, "the mechanical commitment    consistency se-quence" has a missing arrow or symbol.
- Figures are described in captions only. Figure 7-3, a collage of deadline advertisements, is not readable.
- The Notes section gives page ranges that do not match the Contents page numbers.
- Chapter 4 note numbering skips from 12 to 14 in the body text.

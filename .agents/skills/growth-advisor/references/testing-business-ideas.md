# Testing Business Ideas — David J. Bland & Alexander Osterwalder

> A catalog of 44 experiments that turn a business idea into testable hypotheses, and the process to pick, sequence, and read them.

## When to reach for this

- You have an idea and no evidence. You need to pick the first cheap test.
- You know what to test but not which experiment produces the evidence you need.
- You have weak evidence (interviews, surveys) and need to escalate to stronger evidence.
- You are about to build something. Use this to cut the build down or delay it.
- You need to decide: persevere, pivot, or kill.

## Core thesis

- Test ideas before you execute them. Ideas that look great in a plan often fail in reality.
- Break a big idea into small, testable hypotheses. Write each as "We believe that…".
- A good hypothesis is testable, precise, and discrete. Precise means you know what success looks like.
- Hypotheses cover three risks: desirability (customers do not want it), feasibility (you cannot build or deliver it), viability (you cannot earn enough).
- Explore desirability first, feasibility second, viability third.
- Evidence has strength. Facts beat opinions. What people do beats what people say. Real-world beats lab. Large investments beat small ones.
- Confidence comes from three things: strength of evidence, number of data points, number and type of experiments run on the same hypothesis.
- Run several experiments per hypothesis, with rising evidence strength. Do not decide on one weak experiment.
- Insights, not raw evidence, drive decisions. Turn insights into action: persevere, pivot, or kill.
- Reduce uncertainty as much as you can before you build anything.

## The playbook

1. **Shape the idea.** Put the idea on a Business Model Canvas and a Value Proposition Canvas. This breaks the idea into chunks you can test.
2. **Write hypotheses.** Write one hypothesis per sticky note. Use "We believe that…". Make each one testable, precise, and discrete. Also write a few hypotheses that try to disprove your assumptions, to counter confirmation bias.
   - Desirability hypotheses live in: customer segments, value propositions, channels, customer relationships.
   - Feasibility hypotheses live in: key activities, key resources, key partners.
   - Viability hypotheses live in: revenue streams, cost structure, profit.
3. **Map assumptions.** Plot every hypothesis on two axes. X axis: have evidence / no evidence. Y axis: important / unimportant.
   - Top left (important, have evidence): challenge the evidence, then share it and track it.
   - Top right (important, no evidence): this is your test backlog. Start here.
4. **Pick an experiment.** Ask three questions:
   - What type of hypothesis am I testing? Desirability, feasibility, or viability.
   - How much evidence do I already have? The less you know, the cheaper and faster the test.
   - How much time do I have until the next decision point or until money runs out?
5. **Design the experiment.** Write a Test Card with four parts: hypothesis, experiment, metrics, success criteria. Define the who (test subject), the where (test context), and the what (test elements) precisely, so it can be repeated.
6. **Sequence cheap to expensive.** Start cheap and fast, accept weaker evidence, then escalate. Example sequence: interviews, then survey, then simulated sale.
7. **Limit work in progress.** Use a board with Backlog, Setup, Run, Learn. Start with a limit of one item per column. Prefer "Learn" over "Validate" as a column name, so you do not set the bar low and declare victory early.
8. **Run it almost like a scientist.** Keep the evidence clean. Keep the test subject, context, and metrics identical across comparable runs, or the data will not compare.
9. **Read the evidence.** Sort it into weak and strong. Write a Learning Card. Ask: is this a fact or an opinion? Did I watch behavior or hear a claim? Real world or lab? Small or large investment?
10. **Rate your confidence.**
    - Not confident at all: one experiment, weak evidence (one interview or survey series).
    - Not really confident: only interviews or surveys, where people say what they will do.
    - Somewhat confident: several experiments with strong evidence, or one strong call-to-action experiment.
    - Very confident: several experiments, at least one call-to-action test with very strong evidence.
11. **Decide.**
    - Evidence supports the hypothesis: test the next critical hypothesis, or retest the same one at higher fidelity.
    - Evidence refutes it: pivot or kill. A pivot can invalidate earlier evidence and force retesting.
    - Insight unclear: continue testing.
    - Pivot on one of three things: the customer, the problem, or the solution. Hold the other two.
12. **Keep a cadence.** Weekly planning (60 min), daily standup (15 min), weekly learning (60 min), biweekly retrospective (30 min), monthly stakeholder review (60 min). For a solopreneur this is still worth doing alone, with an advisor for the monthly review.

## Rules and heuristics

| Rule | Why | Failure mode if ignored |
|---|---|---|
| Go cheap and fast at the beginning | Early on you know little. You will test more later, so weak evidence is acceptable | You spend time and money proving the wrong direction |
| Run several experiments per hypothesis | Confidence rises with the number and type of experiments on the same hypothesis | You make an important decision on one weak signal |
| Always pick the strongest experiment your constraints allow | Cheap and fast does not have to mean weak | You collect evidence that cannot support the decision you need to make |
| Reduce uncertainty as much as you can before you build | The more expensive the build, the more experiments you need first | You build a product no one wants |
| Write competing hypotheses that try to disprove you | The "We believe that…" format invites confirmation bias | You keep proving yourself right and ignore contrary evidence |
| Make the hypothesis testable, precise, and discrete | You must know what success looks like before you run | You cannot tell whether the result validated or invalidated anything |
| Keep test subject, context, and metrics identical across runs | Otherwise the data does not compare | Messy, uncomparable evidence that cannot be aggregated |
| Limit experiments in progress; start with a limit of 1 per column | Teams underestimate the work in an experiment | Everything slows, and you cannot use the previous result to inform the next |
| Use "Learn", not "Validate", as your board column | Teams set the bar low and declare victory | Fake validation and premature scaling |
| Prefer facts, behavior, real-world settings, and large investments | These predict future behavior best | You act on opinions and stated intent |
| Run the Sean Ellis test only on customers who used the core product at least twice in the past two weeks | Timing skews the answer badly in both directions | Meaningless product/market fit score |
| Ask NPS only after the Sean Ellis test | Referral intent from people who would not miss the product is worthless | You scale on hypothetical referrals |
| Interview in pairs: one interviewer, one scribe | One person cannot ask, listen, and record body language at once | You lose the exact quotes and re-watch recordings at double the cost |
| Debrief for 15 minutes right after each interview | Impressions decay fast | You lose the correction to the script and the body-language signal |
| Time-box analysis; separate reversible from irreversible decisions | Reversible decisions do not deserve long analysis | Analysis paralysis |
| Do not run a 404 test for more than a few hours | It generates real errors for real customers | Customers conclude your product is broken |
| Do not run a Feature Stub for more than 1–3 days | Customers keep expecting the feature to work | You frustrate the users you need |
| Draw a line in the sand for when manual work should be automated | Wizard of Oz and Concierge do not scale by design | You either burn out on manual work or automate prematurely |
| Do not outsource testing | Testing is rapid iteration between test, learn, and adapt. An agency cannot make those decisions for you | You waste time and energy and learn nothing yourself |
| Do not be evil | Experiments can be weaponized as vaporware or propaganda | You scam people and destroy trust |

## Anti-patterns

| Anti-pattern | What it looks like | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Time trap | Not enough dedicated time to test. Multitasking across projects | Carve out dedicated time every week. Set weekly learning goals. Visualize work so stalls are visible |
| Analysis paralysis | Overthinking things you could just test | Time-box analysis. Act fast on reversible decisions. Replace opinion debates with evidence-driven ones |
| Incomparable data | Sloppy definition of hypothesis, experiment, and metrics. Different segments or contexts across runs | Use the Test Card. Make subject, context, and metrics explicit. Involve everyone running it in the design |
| Weak evidence only | Happy with surveys and interviews. Never going deeper into what people do | Do not just believe what people say. Run call-to-action experiments. Get as close to the real situation as possible |
| Confirmation bias | Discarding evidence that conflicts with your hypothesis | Involve others in synthesis. Write competing hypotheses. Run multiple experiments per hypothesis |
| Too few experiments | Deciding on an important hypothesis from one weak experiment | Run multiple experiments. Distinguish weak from strong. Raise evidence strength as uncertainty falls |
| Failure to learn and adapt | Testing forever without deciding | Set aside time to synthesize and adapt. The goal is not to test and learn; it is to decide and progress from idea to business |
| Outsourced testing | An agency runs your experiments | Shift that budget to internal people. Build your own testing capability |
| Artificial validation | Setting the hypothesis bar low so it passes | Rename the board column from "Validate" to "Learn" |
| Fake urgency signals | Fake testimonials, "sold out" labels on unbuilt products, unrealistic claims | Remove them. They are dishonest and they poison the evidence |
| Rebranding a competitor's product as yours | Cloning a competitor, stripping their branding, adding yours | Run Boomerang with the competitor's branding intact |
| Interviewing badly | Talking more than listening, pitching the solution, nodding yes or no, closed questions, back-to-back scheduling | Listen more than you talk. Ask "why". Get facts, not opinions. Leave gaps between interviews. Update the canvas after |
| Premature scaling | Automating a Wizard of Oz before the volume justifies it | Compute the cost per manual delivery and the volume at which automation is cheaper. Do not automate before that |
| Cheap price test | Testing a price you cannot actually deliver at | Work through the cost structure before the mock sale |

## Quotes worth keeping

> "Testing is the activity of reducing the risk of pursuing ideas that look good in theory, but won't work in reality."

Use it to explain to yourself why you are not building yet.

> "The entrepreneur's and innovator's #1 task is to reduce risk and uncertainty."

Use it when prioritizing this week's work.

> "Be mindful that if you create all of your hypotheses in the "We believe that…" format, you can fall into a confirmation bias trap."

Use it when writing your hypothesis list.

> "Your hypothesis is precise when you know what success looks like."

Use it as the bar every hypothesis must clear before you run anything.

> "What people say in an interview or survey is not necessarily what they do in real life or will do in the future."

Use it when someone quotes interview enthusiasm as proof.

> "The most reliable predictor of future behavior is what you observe people doing when they are not aware they are being tested."

Use it to justify split tests and behavioral instrumentation over surveys.

> "You need to run more and stronger experiments if you've only done interviews or surveys in which people say what they will do."

Use it as a confidence check before a build decision.

> "People often think they need to build something to start testing an idea. Quite the contrary. The higher the costs to build something, the more you need to run multiple experiments."

Use it when you catch yourself opening the editor too early.

> "We prefer "Learn" over "Validate.""

Use it when naming your own board columns.

> "It doesn't matter how beautiful your theory is, it doesn't matter how smart you are. If it doesn't agree with experiment, it's wrong." — Richard Feynman

Use it when the evidence contradicts your favourite idea.

> "The goal is not to test and learn. The goal is to decide, based on evidence and insights, to progress from idea to business."

> "Invention is not disruptive. Only customer adoption is disruptive." — Jeff Bezos

## Applies to

- **Idea validation.** This is the core of the book. Assumptions Mapping, then the top-right quadrant, then cheap-to-expensive experiment sequences.
- **Positioning.** Product Box surfaces the messaging customers use themselves. Card Sorting and Buy a Feature rank jobs, pains, gains. Boomerang finds gaps in a competitor's positioning. The Topology case shows customers can be "symptom aware" but not "problem aware", which directs marketing language.
- **Pricing.** Mock Sale is the named tool for finding price points; show real price options, never ask what people would pay. Presale escalates to real payment. Customer Interviews are a starting point for price testing only. Buffer found $5/month by putting three tiers behind a "Plans and Pricing" button.
- **Landing page / CRO.** Simple Landing Page has a full layout recipe: value proposition above the fold, call-to-action email signup below it, then pains, solution, gains. Email conversion averages 2–5%; target 10–15% early. Needs about 100 unique visitors a day. Split Test radically different versions, not increments. Online Ad and Email Campaign drive the traffic. Link Tracking measures the path.
- **Onboarding / activation.** Web Traffic Analysis finds the biggest drop-offs in a flow. Single Feature MVP tests whether the core promise lands. Concierge and Wizard of Oz teach you the real steps to deliver value before you automate them.
- **Retention / churn.** Validation Survey with the Sean Ellis test is the retention-readiness gate: over 40% "very disappointed" before you scale, or you churn people out as fast as you sign them up. Speed Boat surfaces what slows existing users down. Customer Support Analysis and Discussion Forums find unmet needs in the product you already ship.
- **Growth.** Referral Program with explicit targets: 15–20% advocate share, 50–80% friend click through, 5–15% friend conversion. Only give codes to already-passionate customers. Social Media Campaign, Email Campaign, and Online Ad are the acquisition channels. Crowdfunding funds the scale-up.
- **Product decisions.** Feature Stub and 404 Test settle circular feature debates with data. Buy a Feature prioritizes. Extreme Programming Spike answers "can we build it" in 1 day to 2 weeks. Mash-Up says: only write custom code when the assembled services fail or fail to scale.

## Conflicts and limits

- **The source text has real gaps.** The book prints cost, setup time, run time, and evidence strength as icon scales on every experiment page. Those icon values are not in the extracted text. The scales themselves are stated: setup and run time are 1–3 hours, 1–3 days, 1–3 weeks, 1–3 months, 3+ months; cost is under $500, $500–$1,000, $1,000–$10,000, $10,000–$20,000, $20,000+; evidence strength runs 2 to 5. The per-experiment values are not observable from the text. The table above uses the prose statements instead.
- **The desirability / feasibility / viability tag is also an icon.** Every experiment page prints "DESIRABILITY · FEASIBILITY · VIABILITY" with the applicable ones highlighted graphically. The highlighting is lost. The "Tests" column above is filled only where the prose names the risk type, so it is sparse. Do not read a blank cell as "does not test that".
- **Two experiments are documented as short variations only.** Partner & Supplier Interviews and Expert Stakeholder Interviews get one page each, with evidence strength but no cost, setup, or run time prose. 404 Test is likewise a variation of Feature Stub.
- **The book covers testing, not design.** It says so directly. It refers you to Business Model Generation and Value Proposition Design for how to shape the idea in the first place.
- **Benchmarks are stated without sources.** The 15% Feature Stub button conversion, 10–15% landing page email conversion, 15–20% advocate share rate, and 40% Sean Ellis threshold are given as targets with no derivation. Treat them as starting points, not laws. The book itself repeats that conversion rates vary by industry and says to search for your own benchmarks.
- **Contested territory: the 404 Test.** The book includes it and immediately warns it makes your product look broken. Use the decision tree it provides: not mission critical, and you need to go really fast.
- **Contested territory: outsourcing.** The book says outsourcing testing is rarely wise. For a solo founder with no team, this rules out a common shortcut and does not offer a replacement.
- **Sample sizes are small and unjustified.** 15–20 for interviews, Product Box, Speed Boat, Card Sorting, and Buy a Feature. No statistical basis is given. Split Test is the only experiment where the book asks for a confidence level (80% minimum, 98% ideal) and a sample-size calculator.
- **Much of the ceremony and organizational material assumes a company.** Weekly planning, standups, retrospectives, stakeholder reviews, investment committees, and venture-style internal funding. The book gives a solopreneur note for each ceremony, but the funding and committee sections do not apply to a solo founder.
- **Not covered:** technical architecture, hiring, legal structure, fundraising from investors beyond crowdfunding and stakeholder reviews, and any statistical method beyond "use a split test calculator".

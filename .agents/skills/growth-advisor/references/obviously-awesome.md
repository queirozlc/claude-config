# Obviously Awesome — April Dunford

> A step-by-step process to choose the market context that makes your product's strengths obvious to the customers most likely to buy.

## When to reach for this

- Existing customers love the product, but new prospects cannot figure out what you sell.
- Sales cycles run long, close rates stay low, and you lose to competitors late in the deal.
- Churn is high soon after purchase, and new customers ask for features you never planned.
- Customers say your price is too high, and you cannot charge a premium.
- The product changed during development, or the market changed around it.

## Core thesis

- Positioning is the act of deliberately defining how you are the best at something that a defined market cares a lot about.
- Positioning is context setting. Customers use context to decide what a product is and whether to care.
- Every product can be positioned in multiple markets. The default position is rarely the best one.
- The default position comes from two traps: you still think of the product as the thing you set out to build, or the market moved and you did not.
- Positioning is an input to every marketing and sales tactic. Weak positioning weakens everything downstream.
- The traditional positioning statement is a documentation template, not a method. It assumes you already know the answers.
- Your best-fit customers hold the key. Patterns only appear when you filter to the customers who love you.
- Start from the customer's competitive alternatives, not from your feature list.
- The market category triggers assumptions about competitors, features, and price. Choose the category that makes those assumptions work for you.
- Positioning is a business strategy exercise. The business leader must drive it, and every function must buy in.

## The playbook

### The Five (Plus One) Components of Effective Positioning

Define them in this order. Each component depends on the one before it.

1. **Competitive alternatives.** What customers would do if your solution didn't exist.
2. **Unique attributes.** The features and capabilities that you have and the alternatives lack.
3. **Value (and proof).** The benefit that those features enable for customers.
4. **Target market characteristics.** The characteristics of a group of buyers that lead them to really care a lot about the value you deliver.
5. **Market category.** The market you describe yourself as being part of, to help customers understand your value.
6. **(Bonus) Relevant trends.** Trends that your target customers understand and/or are interested in that can help make your product more relevant right now.

Notes per component:

- Competitive alternatives are often not products. Common alternatives are "use a spreadsheet", "hire an intern", "do nothing". In business software the most common alternative is general-purpose software plus manual process.
- Unique attributes can be features, delivery model, business model, expertise, partnerships, distribution, or proprietary process.
- Value must be provable. Your own opinion is not proof. Customer quotes, third-party reviews, and data are proof.
- Target market characteristics must go beyond demographics and firmographics. They must be identifiable enough to build a prospect list.
- A market category is a set of similar products. A trend is a characteristic that is new and noteworthy. "AI" is a trend, not a category. "AI-empowered CRM" names the category and the trend.

### The 10-Step Positioning Process

**Step 1. Understand the Customers Who Love Your Product**
Make a short list of your best customers. They understood the product quickly, bought quickly, became fans, and act as references. Use this list as the reference point for every later step.
If you do not have enough happy customers yet, stop. Keep positioning loose and broad, sell to a wide set, and wait for a pattern.
Position for customers, not for investors. Investors buy your future. Customers buy a fix for a problem they have now.
If you sell one product, position the product and the company as the same thing.

**Step 2. Form a Positioning Team**
Include the person who owns the business of that product. In a startup that is the CEO or founders. Include one or at most two senior people from marketing, sales, customer success, and product. Workshops run well with three to twelve people. Marketing cannot own positioning alone. Consider an outside facilitator.

**Step 3. Align Your Positioning Vocabulary and Let Go of Your Positioning Baggage**
Spend the first hour agreeing on: what positioning means and why it matters, the components of a position and their definitions, and how market maturity and competitive landscape change the style you choose.
Then name your positioning baggage. Ask where your history shows up in your current market, terminology, and feature descriptions. Get the team to suspend their opinions about current positioning for the duration of the exercise.

**Step 4. List Your True Competitive Alternatives**
Answer one question: What would our best customers do if we didn't exist?
Rules: stay focused on the best-fit customer list; rank alternatives from most common to least common; ignore competitors with no mindshare. Group the alternatives into clusters. Teams usually end with two to five groups.

**Step 5. Isolate Your Unique Attributes or Features**
List every capability you have that the alternatives do not. A feature is something your product or company has or does.
List attributes even if some of the team thinks they are negatives. Avoid unprovable claims like "outstanding customer service" and "very easy to use" unless you can prove them. Prefer consideration attributes (what buyers weigh before purchase) over retention attributes (support quality, ease of doing business).

**Step 6. Map the Attributes to Value "Themes"**
For each attribute, write the benefit it enables, then the value in terms of a customer goal.

```
Feature (has or does)  ->  Benefit (what it enables)  ->  Value (maps to a customer goal)
15-megapixel camera    ->  Sharp photo images         ->  Images stay sharp when zoomed or printed large
All-metal construction ->  Stronger frame             ->  Frame lasts 5x longer, saves $50,000/year
One-click reports      ->  Fast report generation     ->  Every part of the org decides on current metrics
24-hour support        ->  Always-available support   ->  Global operations get help in every time zone
```

Then cluster the value points from the customer's point of view. Target one to four value clusters. A single value point is common and acceptable.

**Step 7. Determine Who Cares a Lot**
For each value point, ask: Who cares and why? Repeat until you have identifiable characteristics.
An actionable segmentation lists easily identifiable characteristics that make a buyer care a lot. For businesses: how they sell, what they have already invested in, what skills they lack.
A segment is worth focus when (1) it is big enough to hit your business goals, and (2) it has important, specific, unmet needs common to the segment.
Target as narrowly as you can while still meeting near-term sales targets. Test it: could you hit this year's number selling only to best-fit customers? If no, broaden. If yes, stay narrow.

**Step 8. Find a Market Frame of Reference That Puts Your Strengths at the Center and Determine How to Position in It**

Ways to find candidate markets:
- **Use abductive reasoning.** Ask: what types of products typically have these features? What category typically delivers this value?
- **Examine adjacent (growing) markets.** Look at neighbouring markets, especially fast-growing ones. Only choose one if it makes your strengths obvious.
- **Ask your customers (but be cautious).** They will suggest markets close to where you already sit, and they are not positioning experts.

Then pick a positioning style:

1. **Head to Head: Positioning to win an existing market.** Aim to be the leader in an existing category and beat the leader at their own game.
   - Use when you are already the leader, or when the category is understood but no clear leader exists yet.
   - Rarely right for a new or small business against an established leader.
   - Requires data that the category exists in the minds of a critical mass of buyers, and speed plus capital to claim leadership.
   - Work: reinforce the current buying criteria, prove you deliver them better, defend against emerging criteria.

2. **Big Fish, Small Pond: Positioning to win a subsegment of an existing market.** Carve off a piece where the rules differ enough to give you an edge.
   - Use when the category is well defined, a clear leader exists and it is not you.
   - Requires a definable group with unmet needs, an identifiable prospect list, and a specific important unmet need you meet better than the leader.
   - The subsegment must be big enough for your near-term revenue goal. Test with deal count against segment size.
   - Work: educate the subsegment on how the general-purpose solution fails them, quantify the gap, and still prove you meet the basic category criteria.
   - Risk: the leader adds a "good enough" feature or acquires a rival. Prefer an advantage the leader would damage its broader business by copying.
   - You can expand to adjacent segments later.

3. **Create a New Game: Positioning to win a market you create.** Prove the category deserves to exist, define its parameters, then lead it.
   - Use only after you evaluate every existing category and conclude none puts the focus on your differentiators.
   - Usually needs a large enabling shift: new technology, regulation, economics, or customer preference. Needs a strong answer to "Why now?"
   - Sell the market on the problem first, not on your solution.
   - Hardest style. Needs money, time, and patient investors. Common failure: you build the category, a larger fast follower takes the leadership.

**Step 9. Layer On a Trend (but Be Careful)**
Optional. Aim at the intersection of three things: your product's strengths, your market context, and a trend relevant to your customer base.
Declare the market category first, then the trend. A trend without a category makes you cool and baffling. A trend with no link to your solution is a liability. If no trend fits, skip it.

**Step 10. Capture Your Positioning so It Can Be Shared**
Write a positioning document detailed enough for marketing, sales, and product to use. Break out each component, explain it, and show how the components interact. Add a one-page concise version. Capture the high level on a positioning canvas.

### After positioning: the sales story

Build the sales story before you write messaging. The arc:

```
1. THE PROBLEM your solution was designed to solve.
   "____________________________________________________"

2. HOW CUSTOMERS SOLVE IT TODAY and where current solutions fall short.
   "____________________________________________________"

3. THE PERFECT WORLD - features a perfect solution would have.
   "In a perfect world, ________________________________"

4. INTRODUCE THE PRODUCT in its market category.
   "[Product] is a [market category] for [target customers]."

5. VALUE THEME 1 + how the solution enables it: ______________
   VALUE THEME 2 + how the solution enables it: ______________
   VALUE THEME 3 + how the solution enables it: ______________

6. PROOF: objection handling, case study, customer list.

7. THE ASK: what you want the prospect to do next.
```

Then write one master messaging document. Every campaign starts from it, so messaging does not creep.

### Downstream and maintenance

- Adjust the product roadmap to the new position. The roadmap for "accounting software" differs from the roadmap for "financial services".
- Adjust pricing to the price expectations of the chosen category. A category change can justify a price increase.
- Review positioning every six months, or after a major event in the competitive landscape.
- When a change forces a revisit, restart at Step 4 and check whether the alternatives list shifted in the minds of your best customers.

## Rules and heuristics

| Rule | Why | Failure mode if ignored |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Start from best-fit customers only | Patterns disappear when you average all customers | Muddy survey results and positioning that fits nobody |
| Do positioning only after you have happy customers | The process needs an observable pattern | You lock in a guess and never find the real fit |
| Define components in order: alternatives, attributes, value, characteristics, category, trends | Each component only has meaning relative to the previous one | Positioning that does not match how customers evaluate |
| Define competitors as what customers would use, not who you watch | Customers usually never heard of your rival startups | You claim advantages against a yardstick nobody uses |
| Make every value claim provable with data or third-party opinion | Your own opinion is not proof | Buyers dismiss the claim and you fall back on price |
| Target as narrowly as your near-term sales targets allow | Narrow focus makes advantages obvious and lists buildable | Broad targeting wastes limited sales and marketing budget |
| Choose the category that makes your strengths obvious | The category triggers assumptions about competitors, features, price | You spend marketing budget fighting wrong assumptions |
| Declare the market category before the trend | The category answers "what are you" | Cool but baffling positioning |
| Have the business leader drive the exercise, with every function represented | Positioning is business strategy, not a marketing deliverable | The team ignores the output |
| Check positioning every six months | Products and markets both move | Trap 2: a good product stuck in a stale position |
| Capture positioning in a shareable document plus a canvas | Other teams must act on it | The work stays in one head and decays |

## Anti-patterns

| Anti-pattern | What it looks like | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The traditional positioning statement | Filling blanks in a FOR / IS A / UNLIKE template | Run the 10 steps, then capture the result in the positioning canvas |
| Default positioning | "We built a database, so we are a database" | Step 3: name the baggage, then Step 8: choose the category deliberately |
| Frankencake | "Cake on a stick" - a category where your best features look wrong | Reframe until your differentiators belong ("cake pop") |
| Feature-first positioning | Listing your unique features before you know the alternatives | Step 4 first: ask what customers would use if you did not exist |
| Investor positioning aimed at customers | Website and sales deck talk about disruption and vision | Use customer positioning on all customer-facing assets; keep the investor story separate |
| Unprovable differentiators | "Outstanding customer service", "very easy to use" | Convert to a provable attribute or drop it |
| Trend without a category | "The sharing economy for pets", "Uber for cats" | Name the market first, then layer the trend |
| Trend with no link to the product | Renaming the company after a hot technology with no strategy behind it | Only use a trend that reinforces your actual value |
| Fake subsegment | "Bic for Her" - same product, new label, no unmet need | Prove a specific, important unmet need before subsegmenting |
| Out-cola Coke | A startup going Head to Head with an entrenched leader | Use Big Fish, Small Pond instead |
| Building the category and losing it | You educate the market, a funded fast follower takes leadership | Defend the leadership position at every step, or pick another style |
| Positioning statement in a drawer | Marketing, product, and sales never refer to it | Sales story plus messaging document plus roadmap and pricing changes |

## Applies to

- **Idea validation.** Do not tighten positioning until you have enough happy customers to see a pattern. Sell broadly first, watch which customers love it, then narrow. The "fishing net" rule: position broadly while you have little market experience.
- **Positioning.** The whole book. Five (Plus One) Components, the 10-Step Process, three positioning styles, the positioning canvas.
- **Pricing.** Each market category carries price expectations. Moving to a category with fewer options can support premium pricing. Weak positioning creates price pressure, because a product that looks like everything else cannot charge more.
- **Landing page / CRO.** Name the market category explicitly so prospects know what you are. Lead with value themes, not feature lists. Use provable claims. Use the sales story arc as the page structure: problem, current solutions fall short, perfect world, product in its category, value themes, proof, call to action.
- **Onboarding / activation.** Customer success owns onboarding and account expansion, and both flow from positioning. Well-positioned products attract customers who already understand the value, which shortens time to value.
- **Retention / churn.** Customers who misunderstood your value churn soon after purchase and demand features you never planned. Fix the position rather than build for them.
- **Growth.** Word-of-mouth spreads fastest inside tight subsegments, where one closed deal helps the next. Narrow first, then expand to adjacent segments.
- **Product decisions.** A change in position changes the roadmap. Prioritize features that reinforce the chosen category and value themes. Stop building for badly fitting customers.

## Conflicts and limits

- The book targets B2B technology, most examples come from software with a salesperson in the loop. Self-serve, low-touch, and consumer products get less coverage.
- The process requires a base of happy customers. It gives no method for pre-launch positioning beyond "stay broad and wait for a pattern".
- The advice to start narrow conflicts with investor pressure for a large market. The author acknowledges the tension and separates customer positioning from investor positioning, but does not resolve how to run both stories at scale.
- The author recommends an outside facilitator and a cross-functional team. A solo founder must run Steps 2 and 3 differently, and the book does not say how.
- "Ask your customers what market you are in" is explicitly flagged as unreliable. The book offers no validation test to confirm a chosen category before you commit.
- The book does not cover: message and copy craft (it defers to other books), channel selection, brand identity, pricing models and mechanics, sales process design, or measurement of positioning change beyond anecdotal revenue outcomes.
- Source text issues: the extracted file has systematic OCR ligature dropouts, so "fi" and "fl" vanish in many words ("de ining", "bene its", " igure", "in luencing"). Acronyms carry stray spaces ("C EO", "I BM", "C R M", "LT E"). The traditional positioning statement template on line 993 reads "WHICH PROVIDES competitor's benefits" in both rows, which appears to be an extraction error. A passage in the Head to Head section (around lines 2655 to 2666) is truncated: the "THE BAD NEWS" sentence runs straight into a facial-recognition example with missing opening text. The same section once reads "Bigger Fish, Smaller Pond" where the named style is "Big Fish, Small Pond". Figures and illustrations are not present in the text.

# The Mom Test — Rob Fitzpatrick

> A field manual for talking to customers so they cannot lie to you, and for turning those conversations into facts, commitments, and product decisions.

## When to reach for this

- You have an idea and want to know if a real problem exists before you write code.
- You keep hearing "I love it" and "let me know when it launches" and you have no revenue.
- Your user feedback is inconsistent and you cannot decide what to build next.
- You must find and reach your first customers, and you have no warm network.
- You are running sales or demo calls that end with no next step.
- You must decide who your customer segment is, and every option looks viable.

## Core thesis

- Never ask if your idea is good. Only the market answers that. Opinions are worthless.
- Talk about their life, not your idea. If you do it right they never learn you have an idea.
- Ask about specific events in the past. Anything about the future is an over-optimistic lie.
- Three types of bad data poison conversations: compliments, fluff, and ideas. Deflect, anchor, dig.
- They own the problem. You own the solution. Do not build by committee.
- Truth comes from what people already do and already pay for, not from what they say they will do.
- Commitment is the only proof. Currency is time, reputation risk, or cash.
- Bad news is good news. It is cheap learning. A lukewarm "meh" is more reliable than a "Wow!".
- Product risk and market risk are different. Conversations only reduce market risk.
- A good customer segment is a who-where pair. If you cannot find them, slice further.

## The playbook

### 1. The Mom Test (the three rules)

```
The Mom Test:

1. Talk about their life instead of your idea
2. Ask about specifics in the past instead of generics or opinions about the future
3. Talk less and listen more
```

Apply these to questions that matter. A non-biasing but trivial question still wastes the meeting.

### 2. Sort your questions before you go

Bad questions (they produce fluff, compliments, or false positives):

```
"Do you think it's a good idea?"
"Would you buy a product which did X?"
"How much would you pay for X?"
"Would you pay X for a product which did Y?"
```

Sort-of-okay, only with good follow-ups:

```
"What would your dream product do?"
```

Good questions:

```
"Why do you bother?"
"What are the implications of that?"
"Talk me through the last time that happened."
"Talk me through your workflow."
"What else have you tried?"
"How are you dealing with it now?"
"Where does the money come from?"
"Who else should I talk to?"
"Is there anything else I should have asked?"
```

Fixes for the bad ones:
- Instead of "would you buy": ask how they solve X now, what it costs them, how much time it takes, and what happened the last time X came up. If they have not solved it, ask why not.
- Instead of "how much would you pay": ask how much the problem costs them, what they pay now, and how big the allocated budget is.
- If you are far enough along, replace the price question with an actual request for money. A deposit or pre-order in hand proves the answer.

### 3. Kill bad data in the room

Three types of bad data: compliments, fluff (generics, hypotheticals, the future), ideas.

**Deflect compliments.** Do not accept them as data. Redirect to facts:

```
"Whoops — really sorry about that — I got excited and started pitching. Listen:
you guys seem to be doing a good job in this space — do you mind if I ask how
you're dealing with this stuff at the moment?"
```

Compliment symptoms in the meeting: "Thanks!", "I'm glad you like it."
Compliment symptoms back at the office: "That meeting went really well.", "We're getting a lot of positive feedback.", "Everybody I've talked to loves the idea."

**Anchor fluff.** Fluff has three shapes:

```
Generic claims  ("I usually", "I always", "I never")
Future-tense promises  ("I would", "I will")
Hypothetical maybes  ("I might", "I could")
```

The deadliest fluff is "I would definitely buy that."

Fluff-inducing questions to distrust the answers to:

```
"Do you ever…"
"Would you ever…"
"What do you usually…"
"Do you think you…"
"Might you…"
"Could you see yourself…"
```

Anchoring sequence: fluffy claim -> "When's the last time that happened?" -> "Can you talk me through that?"

**Dig beneath ideas.** Write feature requests down. Do not obey them. Dig for motivation.

```
Questions to dig into feature requests:
"Why do you want that?"
"What would that let you do?"
"How are you coping without it?"
"Do you think we should push back the launch add that feature, or is it
 something we could add later?"
How would that fit into your day?
```

```
Questions to dig into emotional signals:
"Tell me more about that."
"That seems to really bug you — I bet there's a story here."
"What makes it so awful?"
"Why haven't you been able to fix this already?"
"You seem pretty excited about that — it's a big deal?"
"Why so happy?"
"Go on."
```

If you slip into pitch mode, cut yourself off:

```
"Whoops—I just slipped into pitch mode. I'm really sorry about that—I
get excited about these things. Can we jump back to what you were just
saying? You were telling me that…"
```

If they insist on hearing about your idea, promise it at the end of the meeting or an early demo, and say you want to hear their side first so you do not bias them.

### 4. Do not zoom in early

Start broad. Do not focus on one problem area until they raise it or you have hard evidence it is a must-solve.

- Zoom in immediately only if you are 100% certain the problem is a must-solve people already pay for: "What are your big problems with marketing?"
- Otherwise start generic: "What are your big problems right now?" or "What are your big goals and focuses right now?"

Use these to test whether the problem matters at all:

```
"Does-this-problem-matter" questions:
"How seriously do you take your blog?"
"Do you make money from it?"
"Have you tried making more money from it?"
"How much time do you spend on it each week?"
"Do you have any major aspirations for your blog?"
"Which tools and services do you use for it?"
"What are you already doing to improve this?"
"What are the 3 big things you're trying to fix or improve right now?"
```

The signals you want: are they spending money or making money on this, is it in their top 3, are they actively looking for solutions.

### 5. Face the elephant, and name your risk

- Run two thought experiments before every batch: imagine the company failed and ask why. Imagine it succeeded and ask what had to be true.
- Every conversation must include one question that could destroy your imagined business. If an unexpected answer would not change what you do, the question was not important.
- Classify your risk:
  - Product risk — Can I build it? Can I grow it? Will they keep using it?
  - Market risk — Do they want it? Will they pay? Are there enough of them?
- Watch for the burden shift: "If you could get me more gigs, I'd pay you 20%." That is product risk disguised as validation. Marketplaces, ad-supported businesses, affiliate businesses, and video games are heavy on product risk.
- With heavy product risk, conversations prove less. Start building earlier, with less certainty.

### 6. Prepare your list of 3

- Pre-plan the 3 most important things you want to learn from each type of person.
- Keep a separate list of 3 per customer type or partner type.
- Do not agonise over picking the "right" three. Pick the murkiest or most important now.
- You do not need all 3 answers from every person. Pick up where you left off.
- Knowing your 3 lets you use chance encounters. Ask your top question on the spot.

### 7. Keep it casual

- Learning about a customer works better as a quick chat than a scheduled meeting.
- Split problem / solution / sales conversations to avoid bias, but the first one does not have to be a meeting.
- Symptoms of harmful formality: "So, first off, thanks for agreeing to this interview…", "On a scale of 1 to 5, how much would you say you…"
- Duration guide:
  - 5 minutes to learn if a problem exists and matters.
  - 10-15 minutes for a typical early conversation. It can run longer if useful to you and interesting to them.
  - An hour or more to learn the details of an industry from an expert.
  - 30+ minutes once you have a product and a sales feel. Example split: 5 lost to tardiness, 5 hello, 5 on goals/problems/budget, 10 to show the product, 5 on next steps.

### 8. Push for commitment and advancement

- Commitment — they give up something they value: time, reputation, or money.
- Advancement — they move to the next step of your real funnel.

Currencies:

```
Time:            Clear next meeting with known goals
                 Sitting down to give feedback on wireframes
                 Using a trial themselves for a non-trivial period

Reputation risk: Intro to peers or team
                 Intro to a decision maker (boss, spouse, lawyer)
                 Giving a public testimonial or case study

Financial:       Letter of intent (non-legal but gentlemanly agreement to purchase)
                 Pre-order
                 Deposit
```

Signs you are not pushing:

```
A pipeline of zombie leads
Ending product meetings with a compliment
Ending product meetings with no clear next steps
Meetings which "went well"
They haven't given up anything of value
```

Scoring meeting endings:

| Meeting ended with | Verdict | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| "That's so cool. I love it!" | Bad | Deflect the compliment, get back to business. |
| "Looks great. Let me know when it launches." | Bad | Compliment plus stall. Ask for a commitment available today: intro to the boss, tech team, or budget holder; alpha use; a case study. |
| "There are a couple people I can intro you to when you're ready." | Bad | Too generic. Ask who, what "ready" means, and why not now. |
| "What are the next steps?" | Good | Have your next step ready. If you say "let me get back to you", you ruined it. |
| "I would definitely buy that." | Bad | High false-positive risk. Convert to a letter of intent, pre-purchase, deposit, or intros. |
| "When can we start the trial?" | Maybe good | Check what the trial costs them. If it is too cheap, raise the cost: a case study after 2 weeks, a team-wide week of use, or a card on file. |
| "Can I buy the prototype?" | Great | Best possible ending. |
| "When can you come back to talk to the rest of the team?" | Good | Strong B2B signal. No intro means dead end. |

Do not pitch blind. Start even a sales meeting with open-ended learning questions, then confirm by selling.

Spot earlyvangelists. In enterprise software they:

```
Have the problem
Know they have the problem
Have the budget to solve the problem
Have already cobbled together their own makeshift solution
```

Keep anyone who shows deep emotion close. They write the first check.

### 9. Find conversations

Go to them:
- Cold calls and cold LinkedIn messages. Rejection rate is irrelevant. The goal of cold conversations is to stop having them.
- Seize serendipity. Overhear a signal, walk over, ask your top question.
- Find a good excuse to talk about a topic you both care about.
- Immerse yourself where they are. Work the circuit, do favours.
- Landing pages. Treat the emails as leads to contact, not as quantitative validation.

Bring them to you:
- Organise a meetup for your target group. The organiser gets assumed credibility. The author calls this the most unfair trick he knows for rapid customer learning.
- Teach and speak. Conferences, workshops, videos, blogging, free consulting, office hours.
- Blog to your industry. Even with no audience, the blog makes cold emails credible.
- Get clever. One founder hosted a recurring "knowledge exchange" call between department heads of 3 top universities and absorbed their credibility.

Create warm intros:
- Ask your network out loud. Everyone knows someone.
- Industry advisors. The author's first company had 5 advisors at about half a percent equity each, met monthly, producing a fresh batch of intros weekly.
- Professors, especially if you are in or near university.
- Investors, strong for B2B intros.
- Cash in old favours from people who said "let me know how I can help".

### 10. Frame the meeting: Vision / Framing / Weakness / Pedestal / Ask

Mnemonic: "Very Few Wizards Properly Ask [for help]."

```
1. You're an entrepreneur trying to solve horrible problem X, usher in
   wonderful vision Y, or fix stagnant industry Z. Don't mention your idea.
2. Frame expectations by mentioning what stage you're at and, if it's true,
   that you don't have anything to sell.
3. Show weakness and give them a chance to help by mentioning your
   specific problem that you're looking for answers on. This will also clarify
   that you're not a time waster.
4. Put them on a pedestal by showing how much they, in particular, can
   help.
5. Ask for help.
```

Template email:

```
Hey Pete,

I'm trying to make desk & office rental less of a pain for new businesses
(vision). We're just starting out and don't have anything to sell, but want to
make sure we're building something that actually helps (framing).

I've only ever come at it from the tenant's side and I'm having a hard time
understanding how it all works from the landlord's perspective (weakness).
You've been renting out desks for a while and could really help me cut
through the fog (pedestal).

Do you have time in the next couple weeks to meet up for a chat? (ask)
```

Opening the meeting itself: repeat the email, add the referrer as authority, then drop straight into your first question.

```
Hey Tim, thanks so much for taking the time.

As I mentioned in the email, we're trying to make it easier for universities
to spin out student businesses (vision) and aren't exactly sure how it all
works yet (framing & weakness).

I think Tom made this intro (authority) because you have pretty unique
insight into what's going on behind the curtain and could really help us
get pointed in the right direction (pedestal)… (introductions continue)

I was looking at your spinout portfolio and it's pretty impressive, especially
company X. How did they get from your classroom to where they are
now? (grab the reins and ask good questions)
```

Bad framings to avoid: "Can I interview you", "Can I get your opinion on what we're doing?", "Do you have time for a quick coffee/lunch/chat/meeting?"

Use the advisory flip: go looking for industry and customer advisors, not customers. It removes the needy vibe and puts you in control, because you are now evaluating them.

Prefer in person over phone or Skype. Start in person. You lose facial expressions, casualness, and friendship on a call, and friendship is what produces warm intros.

### 11. Customer slicing

Slice a broad segment until you get a who-where pair.

```
Within this group, which type of this person would want it most?
Would everyone within this group buy/use it, or only some of them?
Why do they want it? (e.g. What is their problem or goal)
Does everyone in the group have that motivation or only some of them?
What additional motivations are there?
Which other types of people have these motivations?
```

Then find them by behaviour:

```
What are these people already doing to achieve their goal or survive their problem?
Where can we find our demographic groups?
Where can we find people doing the above workaround behaviours?
```

If a group is un-findable, slice again. Then choose who to start with by who is most:

```
1. Profitable
2. Easy to reach
3. Rewarding for us to build a business around
```

Three ways you end up talking to the wrong people:

```
1. You have too-broad of a segment and are talking to everyone
2. You have multiple customer segments and missed some of them
3. You are selling to businesses with a complicated buying process and have
   overlooked some of the stakeholders
```

Do not just talk to the most senior people. Talk to people who represent your customers.

### 12. Run the process

Prep questions to unearth hidden risks:

```
If this company were to fail, why would it have happened?
What would have to be true for this to be a huge success?
```

The big prep question, if you only have time for one:

```
"What do we want to learn from these guys?"
```

Other prep rules:
- Spend up to an hour writing best guesses about what this person cares about. Not a week.
- Answer anything you can by desk research first. Check LinkedIn and the company site. It takes 5 minutes.
- Prep with the whole founding team, business and product both.

The full process:

```
Before a batch of conversations:
- If you haven't yet, choose a focused, findable segment
- With your team, decide your big 3 learning goals
- If relevant, decide on ideal next steps and commitments
- If conversations are the right tool, figure out who to talk to
- Create a series of best guesses about what the person cares about
- If a question could be answered via desk research, do that first

During the conversation:
- Frame the conversation
- Keep it casual
- Ask good questions which pass The Mom Test
- Deflect compliments, anchor fluff, and dig beneath signals
- Take good notes
- If relevant, press for commitment and next steps

After a batch of conversations:
- With your team, review your notes and key customer quotes
- If relevant, transfer notes into permanent storage
- Update your beliefs and plans
- Decide on the next 3 big questions
```

Who attends: two people is best, one talking and one taking notes. Do not send three. Solo is fine once your notes are concise. Everyone making big decisions, including tech decisions, must attend some meetings. Do not outsource or hire out customer learning until you have a working business model and a repeatable sales or marketing process.

How many: keep talking until you stop hearing new stuff. Often 3-5 conversations for a simple industry and a focused segment. If you pass 10 and results are still all over the map, your segment is too fuzzy.

### 13. Take notes you will actually reuse

Write exact quotes in quotation marks. Use them later in marketing copy, fundraising decks, and to settle team arguments.

Signal symbols (the author uses 12 and invents more in the field):

```
:)   Excited
:(   Angry
:|   Embarrassed
     Pain or problem (symbol is a lightning bolt)
⨅    Goal or job-to-be-done (symbol is a soccer/football goal)
☐    Obstacle
⤴    Workaround
^    Background or context (symbol is a distant mountain)
☑    Feature request or purchasing criteria
＄    Money or budgets or purchasing process
♀    Mentioned a specific person or company
☆    Follow-up task
```

Notes must be:

```
Able to be sorted, mixed, and re-arranged
Able to be combined with the notes of the rest of your team
Permanent and retrievable
Not mixed in with other random noise like todo lists and ideas
```

Media that work: index cards or post-its with one quote per card, Google Docs spreadsheets, Evernote, a dedicated notebook. Media that fail: your general notebook, audio recordings you never sort. Recording is usually allowed if you ask, but it blows a casual conversation.

If notes are inappropriate during the chat, write them immediately afterwards in a corner.

### 14. Self-check: are you just going through the motions?

```
You're talking more than they are
They are complimenting you or your idea
You told them about your idea and don't know what's happening next
You don't have notes
You haven't looked through your notes with your team
You got an unexpected answer and it didn't change your idea
You weren't scared of any of the questions you asked
You aren't sure which big question you're trying to answer by doing this
You aren't sure why you're having the meeting
```

Any persistent item on that list means you are wasting your time.

### 15. Timeboxing

- Prep: about an hour, not a week.
- A batch of customer conversations before you move on product: a week, maybe two. Not months.
- You should be able to answer almost any customer question and move on to new ones within a week.
- If you can shortcut the process by picking up the phone, do that instead.

## Rules and heuristics

| Rule | Why | Failure mode if ignored |
|---|---|---|
| Customer conversations are bad by default. It is your job to fix them. | Nobody else owns the quality of your data. | You collect noise and call it research. |
| Opinions are worthless. | Only the market knows if the idea works. | You build on a stranger's guess. |
| Anything involving the future is an over-optimistic lie. | People are more willing in the imagined future than in the real one. | You forecast revenue from promises. |
| People will lie to you if they think it is what you want to hear. | Your visible ego triggers politeness. | Every answer confirms you. |
| People know what their problems are, but not how to solve them. | Requests carry motivation, not design. | You build a product by committee. |
| You are shooting blind until you understand their goals. | Motivation drives purchase. | You solve a symptom nobody pays for. |
| Some problems do not actually matter. | Implications separate painkillers from vitamins. | You build for a tolerable annoyance. |
| Watching someone do a task shows where the problems really are. | Behaviour beats self-report. | You optimise the wrong step. |
| If they have not looked for ways of solving it already, they will not buy yours. | Active search is the buying signal. | You sell to complainers. |
| They will often show you what it is worth to them. | Current spend is a price anchor. | You price too low or pitch the wrong tier. |
| People want to help you, but rarely will unless you give them an excuse. | Framing unlocks help. | You leave the expert silent. |
| Compliments are the fool's gold of customer learning. | They feel like data and carry none. | You over-invest on applause. |
| Ideas and feature requests should be understood, but not obeyed. | The motivation matters, not the request. | Feature creep, months wasted. |
| If you have mentioned your idea, people will protect your feelings. | Ego on the table blocks truth. | Fluffy mis-truths. |
| Anyone will say your idea is great if you are annoying enough about it. | Pitching buys compliments. | You mistake escape behaviour for demand. |
| The more you are talking, the worse you are doing. | Interruption costs you their mental model. | You lose the insight you came for. |
| You should be terrified of at least one question you ask in every conversation. | Comfortable questions do not de-risk. | You never find the killer flaw. |
| There is more reliable information in a "meh" than a "Wow!". | Lukewarm is honest. | You "up your game" and collect false positives. |
| Start broad. Do not zoom in until you find a strong signal. | Premature zoom manufactures problems. | You validate a non-problem. |
| Learning about customers works better as a casual chat than a formal meeting. | Formality costs time and biases them. | 4 hours spent for 10 minutes of value. |
| Give as little information about your idea as possible while still nudging usefully. | Your idea contaminates their answers. | You lead the witness. |
| Friendly non-buyers are a dangerous source of mixed signals. | Zombie leads look like a pipeline. | You forecast on nothing. |
| If you do not know what happens next after a product or sales meeting, the meeting was pointless. | Advancement is the score. | Endless "went well" meetings. |
| The more they are giving up, the more seriously you can take their kind words. | Currency validates words. | You trust free praise. |
| It is not a real lead until you have given them a concrete chance to reject you. | Rejection is information. | Friend-zoned startup. |
| In early stage sales, the real goal is learning. Revenue is a side-effect. | Learning compounds. | You close one deal and learn nothing. |
| Keep having conversations until you stop hearing new stuff. | Saturation is the stop rule. | You over-research or under-research. |
| Good customer segments are a who-where pair. | Unreachable segments are not segments. | You cannot start. |
| If you are not finding consistent problems and goals, your segment is not specific enough. | Mixed feedback means mixed audience. | 20 conversations with 20 businesses. |
| If you do not know what you are trying to learn, do not have the conversation. | Purpose drives the questions. | Wasted meeting. |
| Notes are useless if you do not look at them. | Retrieval is the point. | Learning dies in a notebook. |
| Kevin Bacon's 7 degrees applies. You can find anyone if you ask a couple of times. | Networks are short. | You assume you cannot reach them. |

## Anti-patterns

| Anti-pattern | What it looks like | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Fishing for compliments | "I'm thinking of starting a business... so, do you think it will work?" | Ask about their life and past behaviour instead. |
| The Pathos Problem (exposing your ego) | "So here's that top-secret project I quit my job for... what do you think?" / "I can take it — be honest!" | Keep your idea and your ego out until you ask for commitments. |
| Being pitchy | "No no, I don't think you get it..." / "Yes, but it also does this!" | Apologise, cut yourself off, return to their last sentence. |
| Being too formal | "Thanks for agreeing to this interview." / "On a scale of 1 to 5…" | Keep it casual. Chat at events, cafes, and meetups. |
| The meeting anti-pattern | Every conversation becomes a calendar block. | Ask your top question on the spot. Save meetings for when you have something to show. |
| Premature zoom | "What's your biggest problem with going to the gym?" to someone who never exercises. | Start with goals and top problems. Zoom only on a signal they raised. |
| Ignoring the elephant | Weeks spent on teachers' dream tool without checking school budgets. | List all failure points. Ask about the scariest one. |
| Burden shift accepted as validation | "If you can get me more gigs, I'll pay you 20%." | Name it as product risk. Test your ability to deliver, not their willingness to pay. |
| Obeying feature requests | "Sync to Excel" goes straight onto the todo list. | Ask why they want it and how they cope now. |
| Accepting "I would definitely buy that" | Fluffy future promise counted as revenue. | Ask for a letter of intent, pre-order, deposit, or an intro. |
| Zombie leads | Nice meetings, no checks, no next steps. | Force a decision. Ask for a today-commitment. |
| Talking to everyone | "Our segment is advertisers" or "students". | Customer slicing until who-where. |
| Talking to the impressive people | Suits on the status report, no time with actual users. | Talk to representative users, even if it looks unimpressive. |
| The learning bottleneck | "You just worry about the product, I'll learn what we need to know." | Prep and review with the whole team. Share exact quotes. |
| Hiring out customer learning | A hired researcher reports back. | Founders attend. Hires may help, not replace. |
| Phone and survey as an escape hatch | Skype scheduled to avoid awkwardness. | Start in person. Use calls as a considered trade-off only. |
| Process obsession | A week of planning how to validate. | Pick up the phone and hack the knot. |

- "Doing it wrong is worse than doing nothing at all." — Use to justify spending time on question quality.
- "They own the problem, you own the solution." — Use when a customer starts designing your product.
- "Compliments are the fool's gold of customer learning: shiny, distracting, and entirely worthless." — Use when a teammate reports "great feedback".
- "The world's most deadly fluff is: 'I would definitely buy that.'" — Use when a meeting note contains this phrase.
- "Ideas and feature requests should be understood, but not obeyed." — Use during roadmap triage.
- "There's more reliable information in a 'meh' than a 'Wow!' You can't build a business on a lukewarm response." — Use when tempted to keep pitching a cold prospect.

## Applies to

- **Idea validation.** This is the book's core. It supplies the question set, the bad-data filters, the risk split of product versus market, and the stop rule of saturation at 3-5 to 10 conversations.
- **Positioning.** Customer slicing produces the who-where pair that positioning needs. The book states that a broad segment waters down your marketing message and causes feature creep.
- **Pricing.** Do not ask what people would pay. Ask what the problem costs them, what they currently pay, and how big the budget is. Current spend gives you a price anchor and lets you choose which alternative you replace, for example a £100/month tool or a £120,000/year agency.
- **Landing page / CRO.** The book is skeptical of landing page metrics as validation. It treats a landing page as a lead generator: email every signup and start a conversation, as Joel Gascoigne did with Buffer. It also says exact customer quotes belong in your marketing language.
- **Onboarding / activation.** Indirect. The trial discussion applies: if trying your product is too cheap, the signal is worthless. Raise the cost of the trial to test seriousness.
- **Growth.** Meetups, teaching, speaking, industry blogging, advisors, professors, and investors are all channels the book gives for reaching customers. Existing workaround behaviour tells you where to advertise, for example bidding on the search terms people already use.
- **Product decisions.** Feature requests get understood, not obeyed. Dig for the motivation. The MTV analytics story shows the cost of skipping that step: three months of work replaced by a scheduled email of a few numbers.

Not covered: retention and churn. The book does not address post-purchase behaviour, cohort analysis, or churn reduction.

## Conflicts and limits

- The book explicitly disagrees with Steve Blank's three-meeting structure from 4 Steps to the E.piphany. Fitzpatrick keeps the problem/solution/sales split for bias control, but says the first one should be a casual chat, not a scheduled meeting. Blank's structure is more rigorous. Fitzpatrick's is faster.
- The book is skeptical of landing page conversion metrics as validation. Growth and CRO books treat those metrics as primary evidence. This is a real conflict. Fitzpatrick's answer is that the emails matter, not the conversion rate.
- The book prefers in-person conversation and warns that phone and Skype damage the power dynamic. The author admits others get good results by phone and says to use what serves you. For a solo SaaS founder with a remote, global customer base, this advice is the weakest part of the book.
- Product-risk businesses get less from this method. For games, marketplaces, ad-supported businesses, and hard technology, the book says conversations only give a starting point and you must build earlier with less certainty.
- Consumer products get weaker signals than B2B. The book says the consumer conversation process does not mimic the purchase process, so enterprise-style advancement signals are not available.
- The 3-5 conversation saturation figure is the author's experience, not a measured result. It is a heuristic, not a statistic.
- The book covers finding the problem and closing the first commitments. It does not cover retention, churn, quantitative experimentation, A/B testing, analytics, or scaling a sales team.
